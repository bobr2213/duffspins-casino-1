How the peg board works

The board is a triangle of staggered pegs, with each row one peg wider than the one above it. A ball dropped from the top has an even chance of moving left or right at each peg, and that choice repeats until it reaches the bottom. After crossing all rows, it lands in one of the pockets along the base — one more pocket than there are rows.

Because each path is built from repeated fifty-fifty turns, balls cluster near the middle more often than the edges. Mathematicians call this a Galton board, and the pattern it creates is a binomial distribution. The payout table simply reverses that curve: central pockets tend to pay less, while rare edge pockets pay more.

Six terms worth knowing

Pocket
The slot at the bottom of the board that holds the final multiplier.
Rows
The number of peg lines a ball crosses; more rows mean more pockets and finer odds.
Risk level
A preset that spreads the payout table wider or tighter without changing the long-run return much.
House edge
The small difference between true odds and paid odds that funds the operator.
Provably fair
A verification system using published hashes so players can check results later.
Variance
The size of the ups and downs around the average result, which is what the risk switch really controls.

Do more rows improve the payout?

No. Extra rows add pockets and stretch the odds curve, so the outer prizes become bigger while their chance drops. The overall return stays broadly the same across board sizes.

None is mathematically superior. Low risk smooths the ride, while high risk puts more value into rare edge hits. Choose the setting that matches your budget and tolerance for swings.
